Reading — How Oil is Formed

Millions of years ago, tiny marine plants and animals died and fell to the ocean floor. Over time, they were buried under thick layers of mud and sand, which hardened into sedimentary rock. The heat and pressure from these layers slowly transformed the organic matter into oil and natural gas. This long process — taking millions of years — is why we call oil a fossil fuel. Today, this oil is found in underground rock formations called reservoirs. To extract it, engineers drill wells into the earth. The oil is then pumped to the surface and sent to refineries, where it is processed into fuels like gasoline, jet fuel, and diesel.

Comprehension Questions

  1. Where did oil originally come from?
  2. What two things caused the transformation of organic matter into oil?
  3. What is a reservoir?

Industry Text — Crude Oil Benchmarks

Read this summary from a professional petroleum industry document, then answer the questions.

CRUDE OIL BENCHMARKS & UPSTREAM PRICING

Not all crude oil is the same. The physical properties of crude — especially its density (measured as API gravity) and its sulphur content — determine how easy and costly it is to refine, and therefore what price it commands on global markets.

Crude oils are broadly classified along two scales: light vs. heavy (light crudes have high API gravity and yield more valuable products like gasoline) and sweet vs. sour (sweet crude has low sulphur content below 0.5% and is cheaper to refine; sour crude requires additional processing).

The most traded benchmark crudes are: Brent Crude (North Sea) — the global pricing benchmark for approximately two-thirds of the world's traded oil; WTI (West Texas Intermediate) — the benchmark for North American crude; and Dubai/Oman Crude — a benchmark for Middle Eastern crude delivered to Asian markets.

Upstream companies involved in exploration and production (E&P) are most directly affected by benchmark prices. When a company produces crude oil, it sells it at a price linked to one of these benchmarks, adjusted for quality differentials.
Reading Comprehension Questions
  1. What two physical properties of crude oil most affect its price and refining cost?
  2. Explain in your own words the difference between 'sweet' and 'sour' crude.
  3. Why is Brent Crude considered the most important global benchmark?
  4. How does the upstream sector relate to crude oil benchmarks?

Context Setting — Case Study

⚠ Deepwater Horizon, Gulf of Mexico — April 2010
The Deepwater Horizon drilling rig suffered a blowout — an uncontrolled release of oil and gas from the well. The blowout preventer (BOP) failed to seal the well. An explosion killed 11 workers, and the resulting oil spill — the largest in US history — released approximately 4.9 million barrels of crude oil into the ocean over 87 days.

How Offshore Platforms Operate — The Well Life Cycle

Offshore oil production is one of the most technically challenging and capital-intensive industrial activities in the world. A modern offshore well goes through five distinct phases:

1

EXPLORATION

Geologists use seismic surveys to identify potential reservoir structures. If results are promising, an exploration well is drilled. Only about 1 in 10 exploration wells results in a commercial discovery. The date drilling begins is called the spud date.

2

APPRAISAL

If oil is found, appraisal wells are drilled nearby to assess the size and quality of the reservoir. Engineers analyse API gravity, sulphur content, and reservoir pressure to determine commercial viability.

3

DEVELOPMENT

The platform or FPSO is designed, constructed, and installed. Development wells are drilled. The wellbore is lined with steel casing and cemented. Blowout preventers (BOPs) are installed at the wellhead.

4

PRODUCTION

Oil and gas flow from the reservoir through the wellbore. A production separator divides the stream into crude oil, gas, and water. Over time, reservoir pressure declines and EOR (Enhanced Oil Recovery) techniques may be applied.

5

DECOMMISSIONING

At end of life, the well is permanently sealed with cement plugs. The platform is dismantled and removed. Decommissioning must comply with strict environmental regulations and costs hundreds of millions of dollars.

Comprehension & Analysis

  1. Why is the exploration phase considered so risky from a financial perspective?
  2. What is the purpose of a BOP, and what happens if it fails? (Reference the case study.)
  3. Why might EOR techniques become increasingly important as existing oil fields age?
  4. Why might decommissioning be challenging? Give at least two reasons.

Language & Professional Output

Passive Voice in Technical Writing

Why Passive?
Technical and scientific texts use the passive voice extensively. It focuses on what is done, not who does it — which is appropriate in professional industry writing.

Active: Engineers install the blowout preventer before production begins.
Passive: The blowout preventer is installed before production begins.

Life Cycle Comprehension

#QuestionAnswer
1Why is exploration risky?Only ~1 in 10 exploration wells finds a commercial discovery; huge capital investment for uncertain returns.
2BOP purpose / failureSeals the well to prevent blowout. If it fails: uncontrolled release → explosion, spill, casualties (Deepwater Horizon).
3Why EOR is increasingly importantAs older fields mature, natural pressure declines; EOR sustains output from existing infrastructure without drilling new wells.
4Challenges of decommissioningRemote/deep locations, enormous cost, hazardous materials disposal, strict regulations, protecting marine ecosystems.
